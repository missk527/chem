Notes (Key points from the speech)

Conservation of mass
  • In a chemical reaction, total mass stays the same.
  • No atoms are created or destroyed — only the bonds change.
  • This is why balanced equations must have the same number of each type of atom on both sides.
Balancing equations (Na + Cl2 → NaCl)
  • Chlorine is diatomic (Cl2), so you start with 2 chlorine atoms.
  • To match this, the products must include 2 NaCl, which then requires 2 Na on the reactant side.
  • Balanced: 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl.
Checking conservation using relative formula mass (RFM)
  • You can test mass conservation by comparing total relative formula masses on each side.
  • Example: 2×23 (Na) + 2×35.5 (Cl) = 117.
  • Products: 2×(23 + 35.5) = 117 → both sides match.
Checking conservation using real masses
  • If 2.3 g of sodium reacts completely with 3.5 g of chlorine gas, you form 5.8 g of sodium chloride.
  • On a balance, the total mass should remain constant throughout the reaction (in a closed system).
Why mass can seem to change when gases are involved
  • Mass may appear to change if a gas enters from the air or escapes into the air.
  • Example increase: heating magnesium in air forms magnesium oxide; oxygen from air adds mass (e.g., 1.0 g Mg → ~1.6 g MgO).
  • Example decrease: decomposing calcium carbonate produces CO2 that can escape, so the measured products seem lighter.
How to avoid the “gas problem”
  • Do the reaction in a sealed container so any gas cannot enter or leave.
  • Then the measured reactant mass (including gases) will exactly equal the measured product mass.
