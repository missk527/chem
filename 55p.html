Potable Water: Treatment, pH, Microbes & Desalination

Notes (rewritten in English from the speech)

What is potable water?
  • Potable water means water that is safe to drink.
  • Potable water is not necessarily pure. In chemistry, pure water contains only H₂O molecules.
  • Potable water often contains small amounts of dissolved substances, so it is usually impure but still safe.
Safe to drink Not always pure Low dissolved solids
Criteria for water to be potable
  • Low levels of dissolved substances (not too much dissolved material).
  • pH between 6.5 and 8.5 (not too acidic or too alkaline).
  • No harmful microorganisms (e.g., bacteria or fungi) present.
Fresh water sources
  • Where potable water comes from depends on location. Many countries have good supplies of fresh water (water with low dissolved content).
  • Rain can collect as either surface water or groundwater.
  • Surface water: lakes, rivers, reservoirs (water exposed at the surface).
  • Groundwater: water stored underground in places like aquifers (permeable rock that holds water).
Surface water vs groundwater
  • Surface water is easy to access and is frequently replaced by rain.
  • However, surface water can dry up in hot, sunny weather because it is exposed to the Sun.
  • In warmer regions, people may rely more on groundwater.
Treating fresh water (3 main steps)
  • 1) Screening: pass water through a wire mesh to remove large objects (e.g., twigs).
  • 2) Filtration: pass water through sand and gravel to remove smaller solid particles.
  • 3) Sterilisation: kill harmful microbes (e.g., bacteria) using one of:
    • Chlorine (bubble chlorine gas through the water)
    • Ozone
    • Ultraviolet (UV) light
Desalination of seawater
  • In drier regions (e.g., parts of the Middle East), there may be limited rain and therefore limited fresh water.
  • Desalination removes dissolved salts to produce potable water from seawater.
  • Seawater is abundant, but desalination methods are often energy‑intensive and therefore expensive.
Two desalination methods
  • Distillation: boil large quantities of seawater, collect the water vapour, then condense it to obtain distilled water.
  • Reverse osmosis: force seawater through a membrane that lets water molecules pass but traps ions and larger molecules.
  • Both methods produce very clean water, but both require a lot of energy.
