Group 1: Alkali Metals

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

Group 1 (Alkali Metals) at a glance
  • Elements: lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K), rubidium (Rb), cesium (Cs), francium (Fr).
  • Unlike many metals, they are soft, have low density, and low melting points.
  • They are very reactive with water, oxygen, and Group 7 (halogens) such as chlorine.
  • Down the group: reactivity increases; melting and boiling points decrease.
  • Cs is so reactive it can ignite at room temperature.
Explaining the reactivity trend
  • All Group 1 atoms have one electron in the outer shell.
  • To become stable, they lose that one electron easily → form +1 ions.
  • As you go down the group, atomic radius increases → outer electron is farther from the positive nucleus.
  • Weaker electrostatic attraction → easier to lose the electron → higher reactivity.
  • Reactivity = how easily atoms lose/gain electrons and react.
Ionic compounds of alkali metals
  • Alkali metals almost always form ionic compounds with non‑metals.
  • Example: Na donates one electron to Cl → both achieve full outer shells → opposite ions attract (ionic bond).
  • Common product: sodium chloride (NaCl), i.e., table salt.
  • Generally, these salts are white solids and dissolve in water to give colorless solutions.
Reactions with water, chlorine, and oxygen
  • With water: metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. (e.g., Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂). From K downward, heat may ignite the hydrogen.
  • With chlorine: heated metal + Cl₂ → white metal chlorides (e.g., NaCl). More vigorous down the group.
  • With oxygen: metals form oxides, but products depend on the element:
    • Li + O₂ → Li₂O (lithium oxide)
    • Na + O₂ → Na₂O (sodium oxide) or Na₂O₂ (sodium peroxide)
    • K + O₂ → K₂O₂ (potassium peroxide) or KO₂ (potassium superoxide)
  • Tip: memorize these oxide/peroxide/superoxide formulas with flashcards.
