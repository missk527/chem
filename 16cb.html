Covalent Bonding: Dot-and-Cross, Displayed & 3D Models

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) Ionic vs covalent bonding recap
  • In ionic bonding, atoms form ions by transferring electrons from one atom to another.
  • This makes oppositely charged ions which are held together by electrostatic forces.
  • The reason electrons are transferred is so that both atoms end up with a full outer shell of electrons.
  • This works well when:
    One atom has too many electrons The other atom has too few
    for example in sodium chloride.
Ionic bonding (quick recap) • Electrons are transferred. • Ions with opposite charges are formed. • Ions are attracted by electrostatic forces. • Each ion has a full outer shell.
2) Why covalent bonding is needed
  • Sometimes we have two non-metal atoms, such as two chlorine atoms.
  • In this case, both atoms need an extra electron to complete their outer shells.
  • Simply giving electrons to each other will not work because they both want to gain, not lose.
  • Instead, they can share electrons so that each atom effectively gets an extra electron.
Covalent bond in Cl₂ • Each chlorine atom needs 1 extra electron. • Each chlorine shares 1 of its own electrons. • Together, 2 electrons are shared. • Each atom now has a full outer shell.
3) Dot-and-cross diagrams
  • The Cl₂ example can be shown using a dot-and-cross diagram.
  • One atom's electrons are drawn as dots and the other's as crosses.
  • This lets us see clearly which electrons originally belonged to which atom.
  • Sometimes you only draw the outer shell of each atom (like in the example), but exam questions may ask for all shells.
Dot-and-cross diagrams • Show electrons as dots for one atom and crosses for the other. • Shared electrons are in the overlapping region. • Always check whether the exam question wants only outer shells or all shells.
4) Displayed formulas and 3D models
  • An easier way to draw many covalent molecules is with a displayed formula.
  • In a displayed formula, we:
    • Write the chemical symbol for each atom.
    • Use lines to show covalent bonds (shared pairs of electrons).
  • Displayed formulas are especially useful for large molecules that would be awkward as dot-and-cross diagrams.
Example: glucose (a sugar) • Large molecule with many atoms. • Displayed formula uses symbols (C, H, O) and lines for bonds. • You do not need to memorise its exact structure, just recognise it as a displayed formula.
  • A limitation of displayed formulas is that they do not show the 3D shape of the molecule.
  • To show 3D arrangement, we use 3D models, which try to represent the actual shape in space.
  • At this level, you only need to be able to recognise these 3D models, not predict the exact shapes.
5) Covalent bonding in ammonia (NH₃)
  • Ammonia has the formula NH₃:
    1 nitrogen atom (N) 3 hydrogen atoms (H)
  • Step 1: Draw the atoms with their outer shells (for our example).
Electron needs • Hydrogen: - First energy level. - Can hold a maximum of 2 electrons. - Each H needs 1 extra electron. • Nitrogen: - Second energy level. - Can hold a maximum of 8 electrons. - Nitrogen needs 3 extra electrons.
  • Step 2: Work out how they can share electrons so that all outer shells are full.
  • Each hydrogen shares one electron with nitrogen.
  • Nitrogen shares one electron with each H, so 3 shared pairs in total.
Resulting bonding in NH₃ • Three N–H covalent bonds. • Each hydrogen has 2 electrons in its outer level. • Nitrogen has 8 electrons in its outer level. • All atoms have full outer shells.
  • This can be drawn as a dot-and-cross diagram or as a displayed formula:
Displayed formula for ammonia • Write N in the centre. • Draw three lines from N to three H atoms. • Each line represents one covalent bond (a shared pair of electrons).
6) Types of substances formed by covalent bonds
  • The molecules we have looked at so far (chlorine, ammonia) are simple molecular substances.
  • In a simple molecular substance:
    • Atoms inside each molecule are joined by strong covalent bonds.
    • However, the forces between molecules are only weak intermolecular forces.
    • These weak intermolecular forces are easily broken.
Examples of simple molecular substances • Chlorine (Cl₂) • Ammonia (NH₃) • Water (H₂O) • Methane (CH₄)
  • Covalent bonds can also form much larger structures:
Polymers • Long chains of repeating units called monomers. • Used to make things like plastic bags and T-shirts.
Giant covalent structures • Huge networks of atoms joined by covalent bonds. • Can involve billions or trillions of atoms in a regular lattice. • Examples: silicon dioxide, diamond, graphite. • Very strong because every atom is covalently bonded.
