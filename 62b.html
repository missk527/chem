Paper Chromatography

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) What paper chromatography is for
  • Chromatography is an analytical technique used to separate the different substances in a mixture so we can identify them.
  • Paper chromatography is a simple type of chromatography often used to separate dyes in ink.
2) Setting up the experiment
  • Draw a baseline near the bottom of a strip of filter paper using a pencil.
  • Add a small spot of the ink/mixture onto the baseline.
  • Place a shallow layer of solvent (e.g. water or ethanol) into a beaker.
  • Stand the paper in the beaker so the solvent touches the paper below the ink spot.
  • Keep the ink spot above the solvent level so it does not wash straight into the beaker.
  • Cover the beaker with a lid to reduce solvent evaporation.
3) What you observe: the chromatogram
  • As the solvent rises up the paper, the dyes dissolve and move with the solvent.
  • Different dyes travel at different speeds, so they separate into distinct spots.
  • The final pattern of spots is called a chromatogram.
  • If a substance is not soluble in the solvent, it will stay on the baseline.
  • Stop the run when the solvent is near the top, then remove the paper and let it dry.
4) Mobile phase vs stationary phase
  • These “phases” are materials, not time periods.
  • Mobile phase = the substance the molecules can move in (a liquid or gas). Here, it is the solvent.
  • Stationary phase = the material the molecules cannot move through. Here, it is the paper.
  • Substances repeatedly dissolve in the solvent (move) and then stick to the paper (pause).
Key idea More soluble in the solvent → spends more time in the mobile phase → moves further/faster. More attracted to the paper → spends more time in the stationary phase → moves less/slower.
5) RF values and identifying substances
  • Distance moved depends on substance properties, but also on how long you run the experiment.
  • So we use an RF value (a ratio) instead of raw distance.
RF value = (distance travelled by the substance) ÷ (distance travelled by the solvent front) Example: dye = 6 cm, solvent front = 10 cm RF = 6/10 = 0.6
  • A pure substance gives one spot with a characteristic RF value (for that setup).
  • Changing the solvent or the paper can change the RF value because you changed the mobile/stationary phase.
  • To identify a dye, compare RF values using the same solvent and paper as the reference data.
