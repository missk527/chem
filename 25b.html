The Mole

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) What a mole means
  • A mole (mol) is a unit for the amount of substance, like metres for distance or seconds for time.
  • It tells you how many particles of a substance you have, not how big the sample looks.
2) Avogadro’s constant and “particles”
  • 1 mole contains 6.02 × 1023 particles.
  • “Particles” can mean atoms, molecules, ions, or even electrons, depending on the substance.
  • This number is called Avogadro’s constant.
3) Why molar mass matches relative mass numbers
  • The mass of 6.02 × 1023 particles of a substance (i.e., 1 mole) in grams is the same as its relative atomic mass (Ar) or relative formula mass (Mr).
  • Examples: 1 mol of C has mass 12 g; 1 mol of O2 has mass 32 g; 1 mol of CO2 has mass 44 g.
4) The key formula (moles, mass, Mr)
  • n = m / Mr where n is moles, m is mass (g), and Mr is relative formula mass.
  • You can rearrange it to find mass: m = n × Mr.
5) Worked examples from the speech
  • Ammonia: Mr(NH3) = 14 + 3×1 = 17. For 42.5 g, moles = 42.5 ÷ 17 = 2.5 mol.
  • Carbon dioxide: Mr(CO2) = 12 + 2×16 = 44. For 3 mol, mass = 3 × 44 = 132 g.
  • Mass of an element inside a compound: in 3 mol of CO2, carbon mass = 3 × 12 = 36 g (so oxygen mass = 132 − 36 = 96 g).
6) Moles in chemical equations (stoichiometry)
  • Balanced equations show mole ratios.
  • Example: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
  • This means 1 mol Mg reacts with 2 mol HCl to form 1 mol MgCl2 and 1 mol H2.
