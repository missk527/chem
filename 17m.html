Covalent Bonding: Simple & Giant Covalent Structures

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) Covalent bonding and types of substances
  • Non-metals can join together by forming covalent bonds.
  • In a covalent bond, atoms share electrons so that each atom has a full outer shell.
  • This can produce:
    Small molecules like Cl2 Small molecules like NH3
  • Small molecules like these are called simple molecular substances.
  • Sometimes, however, non-metals bond covalently to form giant covalent structures such as:
    Diamond Graphite Silicon dioxide (SiO2)
2) Simple molecular substances & intermolecular forces
  • Covalent bonds are very strong, so a lot of energy is needed to break them.
  • Inside each molecule (for example a chlorine molecule), the atoms are held together by these strong covalent bonds.
  • However, when we melt or boil a simple molecular substance, we do not break the covalent bonds.
  • Instead, we only break the weak forces between different molecules.
Intermolecular forces • Weak attractions between separate molecules. • Much weaker than covalent bonds inside molecules. • Only these forces need to be overcome to melt or boil a simple molecular substance.
  • Because intermolecular forces are weak, simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points.
  • Example: chlorine boils at about -34 °C.
3) Boiling point trends in the halogens
  • Individually, each intermolecular force is weak.
  • But the more intermolecular forces there are, the stronger the overall attraction.
  • Going down Group 7 (the halogens) from chlorine to bromine to iodine:
Halogens – trend down the group • Atoms get bigger. • So molecules get bigger. • Bigger molecules → more intermolecular forces. • More intermolecular forces → more energy needed to separate molecules. • Therefore melting and boiling points increase down the group.
  • Approximate boiling points (numbers are examples, you do not need to memorise them):
    • Chlorine: −34 °C
    • Bromine: 59 °C
    • Iodine: 184 °C
  • The key idea: as the molecules get larger, there are more intermolecular forces, so the boiling point increases.
4) Conductivity of simple molecular substances
  • Simple molecular substances do not conduct electricity.
  • This is because:
    • They have no free electrons.
    • The molecules themselves carry no electric charge.
To conduct electricity or heat, a substance must have: • Electrons or ions that are charged • And these charged particles must be free to move
  • Without free-moving charged particles, a substance is a poor conductor of electricity and heat.
5) Giant covalent structures & silicon dioxide
  • Giant covalent structures are made from huge numbers of non-metal atoms.
  • Each atom is bonded to others by strong covalent bonds, forming regular repeating lattices.
Examples of giant covalent structures • Diamond (carbon only) • Graphite (carbon only) • Silicon dioxide (SiO2)
  • Main properties of giant covalent structures:
    • Very strong.
    • Very high melting and boiling points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt them.
    • Generally do not conduct electricity because they have no charged particles, even when molten.
  • Exception: graphite conducts electricity (diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon).
  • Silicon dioxide (SiO₂):
    • Made of silicon and oxygen atoms in a ratio of 1:2.
    • Also called silica.
    • Main component of sand.
    • You do not have to draw its structure, but you should be able to recognise it as a giant covalent structure.
Key comparison • Simple molecular substances: small molecules, weak intermolecular forces, low melting/boiling points, do not conduct electricity. • Giant covalent structures: all atoms covalently bonded in a lattice, very strong, very high melting/boiling points, usually do not conduct electricity.
