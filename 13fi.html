13. Formation of Ions

Key Notes (English)

Adapted from the provided speech. Focus: how ions are formed, which atoms form ions most easily, and how to write ion equations.

1) What is an Ion?
  • An ion is a charged particle.
  • It can be:
    • a single atom, e.g. Na⁺, or
    • a group of atoms (a polyatomic ion), e.g. hydroxide OH⁻.
  • Ions are formed when atoms gain or lose electrons.
2) Why Do Atoms Form Ions?
  • Atoms form ions to achieve a full outer electron shell.
  • A full outer shell makes the atom (or ion) more stable.
  • Forming ions involves gaining or losing electrons, which always involves energy.
  • Atoms that only need to gain or lose one or two electrons need less energy to form ions, so they are more likely to become ions.
3) Group Number and Outer Electrons
  • In the periodic table, the group number tells you how many electrons an atom has in its outer shell (for main-group elements).
  • Examples:
    • Group 1 atoms → 1 electron in the outer shell.
    • Group 2 atoms → 2 electrons in the outer shell.
    • Group 6 atoms → 6 electrons in the outer shell.
    • Group 7 atoms → 7 electrons in the outer shell.
  • To become stable, atoms lose or gain electrons until their outer shell is full.
4) Which Groups Form Ions Easily?
  • Because forming ions costs energy, atoms that only change by 1 or 2 electrons form ions most easily.
  • Group 1 (alkali metals)
    • Have 1 outer electron.
    • Easily lose 1 electron.
    • Form 1⁺ ions (e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻).
  • Group 2
    • Have 2 outer electrons.
    • Easily lose 2 electrons.
    • Form 2⁺ ions (e.g. Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻).
  • Group 6
    • Have 6 outer electrons.
    • Need to gain 2 electrons to fill the outer shell.
    • Form 2⁻ ions (e.g. O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻).
  • Group 7
    • Have 7 outer electrons.
    • Need to gain 1 electron.
    • Form 1⁻ ions (e.g. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻).
5) Groups that Rarely Form Simple Ions
  • Elements in groups 3, 4 and 5 would need to lose or gain 3 or 4 electrons to get a full outer shell.
  • This would require a lot of energy, so they are much less likely to form simple ions.
  • Therefore, we do not usually see simple ions like X³⁺ or X⁴⁻ from these groups in basic examples.
6) Writing Ion Formation Equations
  • We can write symbol equations to show atoms gaining or losing electrons when forming ions.
  • General ideas:
    • If the atom loses electrons and becomes a positive ion, the electrons are written on the right-hand side.
    • If the atom gains electrons and becomes a negative ion, the electrons are written on the left-hand side.
7) Examples – Losing Electrons (Cations)
  • Sodium (Group 1)
  • A sodium atom easily loses 1 electron:
    • Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
  • Magnesium (Group 2)
  • A magnesium atom loses 2 electrons:
    • Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
  • In both examples, the electrons appear on the right because they are being lost.
8) Examples – Gaining Electrons (Anions)
  • Chlorine (Group 7)
  • A chlorine atom gains 1 electron:
    • Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
  • Oxygen (Group 6)
  • An oxygen atom gains 2 electrons:
    • O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻
  • In both examples, the electrons are written on the left because they are being gained by the atom.
9) Charges and Electron Changes
  • If an atom loses electrons, it becomes a positively charged ion (cation):
    • Losing 1 electron → 1⁺ charge.
    • Losing 2 electrons → 2⁺ charge.
  • If an atom gains electrons, it becomes a negatively charged ion (anion):
    • Gaining 1 electron → 1⁻ charge.
    • Gaining 2 electrons → 2⁻ charge.
  • The size of the charge equals the number of electrons gained or lost.
10) Energy and Likely Ions
  • Forming ions always needs energy to move electrons.
  • Atoms that need to move fewer electrons (1 or 2) are more likely to form simple ions.
  • Therefore, simple ions in basic chemistry usually come from:
    • Groups 1 and 2 (losing electrons, forming positive ions).
    • Groups 6 and 7 (gaining electrons, forming negative ions).
