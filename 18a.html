Carbon Allotropes: Diamond & Graphite

Notes (rewritten in English from the speech)

What is an allotrope?
  • An allotrope is a different structural form of the same element in the same physical state.
  • In the solid state, carbon can exist as diamond, graphite, graphene, and fullerenes (and others).
Allotrope Same element Different structure
Giant covalent structures
  • Both diamond and graphite are giant covalent structures.
  • They are made from a regular lattice of atoms joined by many strong covalent bonds, so the structures are generally very strong.
Diamond: structure and properties
  • In diamond, each carbon atom forms 4 covalent bonds (the maximum carbon can make).
  • This creates a rigid 3D tetrahedral network (a repeating 3D pattern).
  • Because the covalent bonds are very strong, diamond is very hard and has a very high melting point.
  • Diamond does not conduct electricity because it has no free electrons (and no mobile ions) to carry charge.
Graphite: structure and properties
  • In graphite, each carbon atom forms 3 covalent bonds.
  • The atoms are arranged in hexagons forming large, flat sheets.
  • Many sheets stack to make layers, but the layers are held together only by weak forces (no covalent bonds between layers).
  • Layers can slide over each other, so graphite is soft and slippery compared with diamond.
  • Graphite still has a high melting point because the covalent bonds within each sheet are strong.
Why graphite conducts
  • Carbon can form 4 covalent bonds, but in graphite it only uses 3.
  • Each carbon atom has one spare electron that becomes delocalised (free to move across the sheet).
  • These delocalised electrons allow graphite to conduct electricity and also conduct heat well.
Graphene and fullerenes
  • A single layer of graphite is called graphene.
  • Scientists can isolate graphene layers and use them to create other carbon structures, such as tubes and spheres.
  • These carbon structures are called fullerenes (explored in the next video).
