Ionic Bonding & Dot-and-Cross Diagrams

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) Recap: ions and stability
  • Ions are charged particles formed when atoms lose or gain electrons.
  • If an atom loses electrons it becomes a positive ion (cation); if it gains electrons it becomes a negative ion (anion).
  • Atoms tend to gain or lose electrons in order to get a full outer shell of electrons – this makes them more stable.
  • We can show ion formation using half-equations, for example:
    Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (sodium atom loses one electron) Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (chlorine atom gains one electron)
2) Electron transfer & formation of an ionic bond
  • In real reactions, electron transfer doesn’t happen in isolation – it happens between metal and non-metal atoms.
  • Example: sodium has one electron in its outer shell that it “wants” to lose; chlorine has seven outer electrons and “wants” one more.
  • The outer electron from sodium is transferred to chlorine:
    Na + Cl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻
  • Both particles now have full outer shells, but they are charged ions.
  • Sodium forms a 1+ ion (Na⁺); chloride forms a 1− ion (Cl⁻).
  • Oppositely charged ions are attracted to each other by strong electrostatic forces.
  • This electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions is called an ionic bond.
3) Dot-and-cross diagrams for ionic bonding
  • A dot-and-cross diagram shows electrons as dots for one atom and crosses for the other, so we can see which electrons came from which atom.
  • We show the movement of electrons with an arrow from the atom losing the electron to the atom gaining it.
  • After the transfer, each ion is drawn inside square brackets with its charge in the top right corner:
    [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻
  • In full diagrams we can draw all shells of each atom, but often the exam only asks for the outer shell, which saves time.
4) Example: sodium chloride (NaCl)
  • Sodium has 1 outer electron; chlorine has 7 outer electrons.
  • One electron moves from sodium to chlorine.
  • The result is:
    Na atom → Na⁺ ion (1+ charge, lost 1 electron) Cl atom → Cl⁻ ion (1− charge, gained 1 electron)
  • These ions form the ionic compound sodium chloride.
5) Harder example: magnesium chloride (MgCl₂)
  • Magnesium has two electrons in its outer shell.
  • Each chlorine atom has seven outer electrons and needs one more to get a full outer shell.
  • In MgCl₂ there is one magnesium atom and two chlorine atoms.
  • Magnesium loses two electrons, one to each chlorine atom.
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (magnesium loses 2 electrons) Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (each chlorine gains 1 electron) Overall: Mg + 2Cl → Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻
  • The final ions have charges:
    Mg²⁺ (magnesium 2+ ion) 2 × Cl⁻ (two chloride 1− ions)
  • In a dot-and-cross diagram for MgCl₂ (outer shells only):
    • Show Mg²⁺ in brackets with no outer-shell electrons.
    • Show each Cl⁻ in brackets with a full outer shell (8 electrons).
    • Place the two chloride ions on either side of the magnesium ion, to reflect the attraction between them.
6) Key exam reminders
  • Always show charges clearly on ions (e.g. 1+, 2+, 1−).
  • Use dots for electrons from one atom and crosses for electrons from the other atom.
  • Draw square brackets around each ion in the final diagram.
  • Include arrows to show the direction of electron transfer.
  • Remember: metal → positive ion, non-metal → negative ion.
