Limiting Reactants — Excess Reactants & Product Mass

Notes (rewritten in English from the given speech)

What a limiting reactant is
  • A limiting reactant is the reactant that gets used up first in a chemical reaction.
  • Once it is fully consumed, the reaction stops, so it limits the amount of product that can be formed.
  • A reactant that remains after the reaction ends is called an excess reactant.
Limiting reactantExcess reactantProduct mass
Example: hydrochloric acid and calcium carbonate
  • Hydrochloric acid reacting with calcium carbonate produces calcium chloride, water, and carbon dioxide.
  • As the reaction happens, the solid calcium carbonate gradually disappears and bubbles of gas are released.
  • The bubbles are carbon dioxide, showing that a reaction is taking place.
  • When the fizzing slows down and finally stops, one reactant must have been completely used up.
  • If the calcium carbonate has all disappeared, then calcium carbonate is the limiting reactant.
  • The hydrochloric acid is likely still left over, so it is in excess.
Why the limiting reactant matters
  • The amount of product formed depends entirely on the limiting reactant.
  • If more calcium carbonate is added, more fizzing occurs because more products can be made.
  • If more hydrochloric acid is added after the calcium carbonate is gone, nothing happens because there is no calcium carbonate left to react.
Recognizing excess reactants in calculations
  • Sometimes a question directly says one reactant is in excess.
  • Sometimes it is implied. For example, when sodium is burned in air, oxygen is assumed to be in excess because there is plenty of oxygen in the air.
  • That means sodium is the limiting reactant in the calculation.
Worked example: 115 g of sodium making sodium oxide
  • Start by writing and balancing the equation: 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O.
  • Find moles of sodium using moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  • 115 ÷ 23 = 5, so there are 5 moles of sodium.
  • Use the balanced equation to find the ratio between sodium and sodium oxide: 4:2, which simplifies to 2:1.
  • If 5 moles of sodium react, they produce 2.5 moles of sodium oxide.
  • Now find the Mr of sodium oxide: (2 × 23) + 16 = 62.
  • Use mass = moles × Mr to calculate the product mass.
  • 2.5 × 62 = 155 g.
  • So, the mass of sodium oxide produced is 155 g.

Key idea: the product mass is controlled by the limiting reactant, not by the reactant present in excess.
