53. Cracking of Hydrocarbons & Alkenes

Key Notes (English)

Adapted from the provided speech. Focus: why we crack long-chain hydrocarbons, how cracking works, how to write cracking equations, and the key properties and uses of alkenes.

1) Crude Oil, Hydrocarbons and Fractional Distillation
  • Crude oil is a mixture of many different hydrocarbons.
  • Hydrocarbons are molecules made only from carbon and hydrogen atoms.
  • We can separate crude oil using fractional distillation, based on different chain lengths and boiling points.
  • Short-chain hydrocarbons are the most flammable and make the best fuels.
  • Long-chain hydrocarbons are thick, viscous liquids and are less useful as fuels.
2) What Is Cracking?
  • Cracking is a process used to break down long, less useful hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful ones.
  • It converts long-chain alkanes into shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes.
  • The shorter hydrocarbons tend to be more flammable, so they make better fuels.
3) Cracking as Thermal Decomposition
  • Cracking is a type of thermal decomposition reaction.
  • Thermal means heating.
  • Decomposition means breaking down a substance into simpler substances.
  • So thermal decomposition means breaking down molecules by heating them.
4) First Step in Cracking – Vaporising the Hydrocarbons
  • Whether we use catalytic cracking or steam cracking, the first step is the same.
  • The long-chain hydrocarbons are heated until they vaporise.
  • Vaporising means turning the liquid hydrocarbons into a gas.
5) Catalytic Cracking
  • In catalytic cracking, the hydrocarbon vapour is passed over a hot catalyst.
  • The catalyst is often powdered aluminium oxide.
  • As the long-chain hydrocarbon vapour comes into contact with the catalyst, the molecules split apart.
  • This produces two or more smaller hydrocarbons.
6) Steam Cracking
  • In steam cracking, the hydrocarbon vapour is mixed with steam.
  • The mixture is then heated to a very high temperature.
  • The intense heat causes the long hydrocarbon chains to break into shorter ones.
  • Again, the products include shorter alkanes and alkenes.
7) Cracking Equations – Balancing Atoms
  • In cracking, a long-chain alkane is broken into a shorter alkane plus an alkene.
  • Example: decane (C₁₀H₂₂) can crack to form a shorter alkane (such as heptane, C₇H₁₆) and an alkene (such as propene, C₃H₆).
  • The total number of carbon atoms on each side of the equation must be the same.
  • The total number of hydrogen atoms must also be the same.
  • This is the same idea as for any other balanced chemical equation.
8) Worked Example – Decane to Ethene and an Alkane
  • Consider the cracking: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₂H₄ + ?
  • We know one product is ethene (C₂H₄), an alkene.
  • To find the other alkane, subtract the atoms in C₂H₄ from C₁₀H₂₂.
  • Carbons: 10 − 2 = 8, so the alkane must have 8 carbons.
  • Hydrogens: 22 − 4 = 18, so it must have 18 hydrogens.
  • The other product is therefore C₈H₁₈, an alkane.
9) Alkanes vs Alkenes – Saturated and Unsaturated
  • Alkanes and alkenes are both examples of homologous series – families of compounds with similar properties and the same general formula style.
  • Alkanes have only single bonds between carbon atoms, so they are saturated.
  • Alkenes contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond, so they are unsaturated.
10) Properties and Uses of Alkenes
  • Because of the double bond, alkenes are generally more reactive than alkanes.
  • Alkenes can react with bromine; for example, bromine water is decolourised from orange to colourless by an alkene.
  • This colour change is the test for alkenes you need to remember.
  • Alkenes can also be joined together in addition reactions to form polymers.
  • In polymerisation, the double bond breaks and each carbon forms new bonds to neighbouring molecules, creating long polymer chains.
