15. Ionic Compounds

Key Notes (English)

Adapted from the provided speech. Focus: what ionic compounds are, how they are structured, their properties, and how to work out their formulas.

1) What Is Ionic Bonding?
  • Ionic bonding usually happens between a metal and a non‑metal.
  • A metal atom (for example, sodium) loses one or more electrons.
  • A non‑metal atom (for example, chlorine) gains those electrons.
  • This forms oppositely charged ions (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻) that attract each other.
  • The strong electrostatic attraction between the positive and negative ions is called an ionic bond.
2) Ionic Compounds in Real Life
  • An ionic compound is made of huge numbers of ions, not just one pair.
  • Positive and negative ions alternate so each ion is attracted to many others around it.
  • This repeating arrangement in all directions forms a giant three‑dimensional lattice.
  • The lattice is regular and highly ordered, giving ionic solids a crystalline structure.
3) Representing Ionic Lattice Structures
  • In diagrams, different ions can be drawn with different colours (for example, Na⁺ green, Cl⁻ red).
  • We often use ball‑and‑stick models to show the positions of ions and how they are connected.
  • The “sticks” show the ionic bonds, but remember that in reality the attraction is in all directions, not just along the sticks.
4) Melting and Boiling Points of Ionic Compounds
  • Ionic compounds usually have very high melting and boiling points.
  • This is because there are many strong ionic bonds throughout the lattice.
  • To melt or boil the solid, we must provide enough energy to break all these bonds.
  • That requires very high temperatures, so ionic solids are often solid at room temperature.
5) Electrical Conductivity
  • To conduct electricity, a substance needs charged particles that can move (ions or electrons).
  • In a solid ionic compound, the ions are fixed in place in the lattice, so they cannot move.
  • Therefore, solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity.
  • When ionic compounds are melted or dissolved in water, the ions are free to move.
  • In these liquid or aqueous states, the movement of ions means they can conduct electricity.
6) Simple Ionic Formulas – Sodium Chloride
  • When forming an ionic compound, the total positive charge must balance the total negative charge.
  • Sodium forms a 1⁺ ion (Na⁺) and chloride forms a 1⁻ ion (Cl⁻).
  • One Na⁺ and one Cl⁻ balance to give an overall neutral compound.
  • Therefore the formula for sodium chloride is NaCl.
7) Group 2 Example – Magnesium Chloride
  • Magnesium is in group 2 of the periodic table.
  • It loses two electrons to form a 2⁺ ion, Mg²⁺.
  • Chloride is still a 1⁻ ion, Cl⁻.
  • To balance the 2⁺ charge of Mg²⁺, we need two chloride ions (2 × 1⁻ = 2⁻).
  • So the neutral formula is MgCl₂ (one Mg²⁺ and two Cl⁻).
8) Important Polyatomic Ions to Remember
  • Some ions are not single elements; they are groups of atoms that stay together.
  • You must memorise their formulas and charges because they are not obvious from the periodic table.
  • Key examples:
    • Hydroxide ion: OH⁻
    • Sulfate ion: SO₄²⁻
    • Nitrate ion: NO₃⁻
    • Carbonate ion: CO₃²⁻
    • Ammonium ion: NH₄⁺
9) Calcium Hydroxide – Using Brackets
  • Calcium is in group 2, so it forms a 2⁺ ion, Ca²⁺.
  • Hydroxide is a 1⁻ ion, OH⁻.
  • To balance Ca²⁺, we need two hydroxide ions (2 × 1⁻ = 2⁻).
  • The formula is therefore Ca(OH)₂.
  • We use brackets around OH because the little “2” applies to the whole hydroxide ion, not just to hydrogen.
10) Aluminium Sulfate – Finding the Lowest Common Multiple
  • Aluminium is in group 3, so it forms a 3⁺ ion, Al³⁺.
  • Sulfate is a 2⁻ ion, SO₄²⁻.
  • We need a combination of Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ so that the total positive and negative charges are equal.
  • The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
  • To get 6⁺, we need two Al³⁺ ions (2 × 3⁺ = 6⁺).
  • To get 6⁻, we need three SO₄²⁻ ions (3 × 2⁻ = 6⁻).
  • So the formula is Al₂(SO₄)₃, with brackets to show that the “3” applies to the whole sulfate ion.
