41C. Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

0:00 - 0:36 | Introduction to Electrolysis
  • Electrolysis is used to split compounds into elements using electricity.
  • An electric current passes through an electrolyte.
  • Ions must be free to move for electrolysis to work.
  • Insoluble compounds are melted to form molten liquids.
  • Soluble compounds can dissolve in water to make aqueous solutions.
0:43 - 1:26 | Electrolysis Equipment and Ions
  • An electrolysis setup uses a beaker, electrodes, wires, and a power supply.
  • The positive electrode is the anode.
  • The negative electrode is the cathode.
  • Aqueous solutions contain ions from the compound and from water.
  • Water provides hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions.
1:33 - 2:20 | Rules for the Cathode
  • The cathode attracts positive ions.
  • Positive ions include metal ions and hydrogen ions.
  • Only one positive ion is discharged.
  • The less reactive element is discharged at the cathode.
  • Hydrogen is discharged instead of reactive metals.
  • Copper ions discharge because copper is less reactive than hydrogen.
2:20 - 2:56 | Rules for the Anode
  • The anode attracts negative ions.
  • Negative ions include hydroxide ions and non-metal ions.
  • If halide ions are present, they are discharged.
  • Halides include chloride, bromide, and fluoride.
  • If no halide is present, hydroxide ions are discharged.
2:56 - 4:34 | Copper Sulfate Example
  • Aqueous copper sulfate contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, and OH⁻ ions.
  • At the cathode, copper ions gain electrons.
  • Solid copper forms around the cathode.
  • At the anode, hydroxide ions lose electrons.
  • Hydroxide ions form oxygen gas and water.
  • Oxygen is released at the anode.
4:34 - 5:18 | Sodium Chloride Example
  • Aqueous sodium chloride contains Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, and OH⁻ ions.
  • Hydrogen ions discharge at the cathode.
  • Hydrogen gas forms at the cathode.
  • Chloride ions discharge at the anode.
  • Chlorine gas forms at the anode.
