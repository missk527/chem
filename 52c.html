Crude Oil & Fractional Distillation

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) What is crude oil?
  • Crude oil is a fossil fuel obtained from deep underground.
  • It is a mixture of many different compounds.
  • Almost all of these compounds are hydrocarbons – molecules made only of hydrogen and carbon.
  • The most common hydrocarbons in crude oil are alkanes.
Hydrocarbon = compound containing only hydrogen and carbon. Alkanes = simplest hydrocarbon family (e.g. methane, ethane, propane, butane).
2) How is crude oil formed?
  • Crude oil is formed naturally from the remains of dead plants and animals, especially plankton.
  • These remains were buried in mud on the seabed millions of years ago.
  • Over millions of years, high pressure and high temperature turned this organic material into crude oil.
  • The oil soaked into the surrounding rocks and was stored underground.
  • We recover it by drilling into the rock and pumping it up to the surface.
3) Crude oil as a non-renewable resource
  • Crude oil takes a very long time to form (millions of years).
  • We are using it up much faster than it is being made.
  • This means crude oil is effectively a finite resource – if we keep using it at the current rate, one day it will run out.
  • Fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas are therefore called non-renewable fuels.
4) Why do we fractionally distil crude oil?
  • Crude oil is a mixture of many different hydrocarbons with different properties.
  • We want to separate them so we can use each fraction for different purposes (fuels, roads, chemicals, etc.).
  • We do this using fractional distillation.
Fractional distillation: • Heats crude oil and separates it into fractions. • Fractions contain hydrocarbons with similar boiling points.
5) How fractional distillation works
  • Step 1: Feed the crude oil into a chamber and heat it until most of it evaporates (turns into gas).
  • Step 2: The hot gaseous mixture enters a fractionating column.
  • The column is:
    • Very hot at the bottom.
    • Cooler towards the top.
  • As the gases rise up the column, they cool down.
  • When a gas reaches a part of the column that is cooler than its boiling point, it condenses back into a liquid.
  • Liquids are collected at different levels in the column – these are the different fractions.
Key idea: • Different hydrocarbons have different boiling points. • This allows separation as they condense at different heights.
6) Long-chain vs short-chain hydrocarbons
  • Longer-chain hydrocarbons (more carbon atoms):
    • Have higher boiling points.
    • Condense back into liquids near the bottom of the column.
    • Often form thick, heavy liquids or solids.
Examples of fractions near the bottom: • Bitumen – used to surface roads. • Heavy fuel oil – can be separated further for heating oil, fuel oil, lubricating oil.
  • Shorter-chain hydrocarbons (fewer carbons):
    • Have lower boiling points.
    • Stay as gases for longer and rise higher up the column.
    • Only condense near the top where it is cooler.
Examples of shorter-chain fractions: • Diesel and petrol – fuels for cars and other road vehicles. • Kerosene – fuel for jet engines. • LPG (liquefied petroleum gas) – mainly propane and butane; so short they can remain gases.
  • Fractions nearer the top (short chains) are usually the most flammable and make the best fuels.
  • Long-chain hydrocarbons at the bottom are often poor fuels.
  • Longer chains can be cracked into shorter, more useful hydrocarbons (covered in another lesson).
7) Petrochemicals and feedstock
  • The substances we obtain from crude oil are called petrochemicals.
  • As well as being used directly as fuels or road surfaces, petrochemicals are important feedstock.
Feedstock = raw materials used by the chemical industry. Examples of products made from petrochemical feedstock: • Solvents • Lubricants • Polymers (plastics) • Detergents

Fractional distillation of crude oil is therefore fundamental to modern industry and transport.
