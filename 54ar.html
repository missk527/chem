Reactions of Alkenes: Addition & Bromine Test

Key Notes (rewritten from the speech)

1) Alkenes as unsaturated hydrocarbons
  • Alkenes are described as unsaturated hydrocarbons.
  • Unsaturated means they contain at least one C=C double bond.
  • This carbon–carbon double bond is the functional group of alkenes.
  • Hydrocarbon means the molecule contains only carbon and hydrogen atoms.
Alkenes • Unsaturated hydrocarbons • Contain at least one C=C double bond (functional group) • Only carbon and hydrogen atoms
2) Addition reactions of alkenes
  • The key idea is that alkenes can undergo addition reactions.
  • In an addition reaction, the C=C double bond opens up.
  • The two carbon atoms can then bond to atoms from another molecule.
Why alkenes do addition reactions • The double bond can "open up". • The two carbons each gain a new single bond. • Atoms from another molecule are added across the double bond.
  • This allows us to add another molecule to the alkene.
  • We will look at three main addition reactions:
    With hydrogen (H₂) With water / steam (H₂O) With halogens (e.g. bromine)
3) Addition of hydrogen – making alkanes
  • Example alkene: propene (three-carbon alkene).
  • If we add hydrogen gas and use a catalyst, the C=C double bond breaks open.
  • Each carbon from the double bond then bonds to a hydrogen atom.
Hydrogenation of an alkene • Propene (alkene) + hydrogen (H₂) → propane (alkane) • Double bond becomes a single bond. • Product is now saturated.
  • The product propane has no double bond, so it is a saturated alkane, not an alkene.
4) Addition of water (steam) – making ethanol
  • Example alkene: ethene (two-carbon alkene).
  • Conditions:
    • a catalyst,
    • high temperature so water is present as steam.
  • The C=C double bond opens up again.
  • The water molecule splits into H and OH (an -O-H group) which add across the double bond.
Hydration of ethene • Ethene + water (steam) → ethanol • One carbon gains H, the other gains an OH group. • Ethanol is an alcohol (it contains an –OH group).
  • Ethanol is the alcohol found in alcoholic drinks and in many industrial processes.
  • This reaction is therefore very important in industry.
Separating products • After the reaction we have: - ethanol (desired product) - unreacted ethene - water • Ethene has a relatively low boiling point, so it stays as a gas when cooled, while water and ethanol condense. • Water and ethanol can then be separated by fractional distillation.
5) Fractional distillation of ethanol and water
  • To separate ethanol from water, we use fractional distillation.
  • A mixture of ethanol and water is placed in a round-bottom heating flask.
Fractional distillation steps • Heat the mixture (e.g. with a Bunsen burner). • Ethanol has a slightly lower boiling point than water. • Ethanol boils and evaporates first. • Ethanol vapour rises up the fractionating column. • It then condenses and is collected in a separate beaker. • Water stays in the flask because it does not boil yet.
6) Addition of halogens – bromine water test
  • Alkenes also react with halogens such as bromine.
  • This reaction is very similar to addition with hydrogen, but no catalyst is needed.
  • The most common example is the reaction of ethene with bromine.
Reaction with bromine • Bromine solution is orange. • Ethene + bromine → dibromoethane. • The C=C double bond opens and each carbon bonds to a bromine atom. • The bromine is used up, so the orange colour disappears.
  • This is the main test for alkenes:
    • Alkenes decolourise bromine water from orange to colourless.
    • Alkanes are saturated and have no double bond, so they do not react with bromine under these conditions.
    • If we add bromine water to an alkane, the solution stays orange.
Summary of bromine test • Alkenes: orange bromine water → colourless (because they react). • Alkanes: orange bromine water stays orange (no reaction).
