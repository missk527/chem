51. Alkanes: Properties & Combustion

Key Notes (English)

Adapted from the provided speech. Focus: how the properties of alkanes change with chain length, and how to write balanced equations for complete combustion.

1) Recap – What Are Alkanes?
  • Alkanes are a homologous series of hydrocarbons.
  • They contain only carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) atoms.
  • Alkanes have no double bonds between carbon atoms (only single C–C bonds).
  • Examples include the first four: methane, ethane, propane, butane.
2) General Property Trends with Chain Length
  • All alkanes have similar chemical properties, but there are trends as the carbon chain length increases.
  • Key trend: Boiling point increases as chain length increases.
  • Shorter alkanes (with 1–4 carbons) have low boiling points and are usually gases at room temperature.
  • Longer alkanes (more than four carbons) tend to be liquids at room temperature, and very long chains can be solids.
3) Volatility and Viscosity
  • Volatile means a substance evaporates easily.
  • Shorter alkanes are more volatile because of their lower boiling points.
  • Viscous means a liquid is thick and sticky (like honey).
  • Longer alkanes are more viscous – they flow slowly and feel “thicker”.
4) Flammability of Alkanes
  • Flammable means a substance is easy to ignite or burn.
  • Shorter alkanes are more flammable than longer ones.
  • This makes short-chain alkanes very useful as fuels.
  • We will see this again when studying crude oil and its fractions.
5) Combustion of Alkanes – General Idea
  • One of the main uses of hydrocarbons like alkanes is as fuels.
  • When they burn in oxygen, they release a lot of energy.
  • If there is enough oxygen, the alkane undergoes complete combustion.
  • Complete combustion products:
    • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
    • Water (H₂O)
  • These reactions are exothermic – they release energy to the surroundings.
6) Oxidation in Combustion
  • During complete combustion, both carbon and hydrogen in the hydrocarbon are oxidised.
  • Carbon combines with oxygen to form CO₂.
  • Hydrogen combines with oxygen to form H₂O.
  • So we can say the carbon and hydrogen atoms are being oxidised by the oxygen.
7) Writing Combustion Equations – General Form
  • For a hydrocarbon like an alkane, complete combustion always fits the pattern:
  • hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
  • In symbols, for an alkane CₓHᵧ:
    • CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  • In exams you must be able to write and balance these equations.
8) Example 1 – Complete Combustion of Propane
  • Propane has the formula C₃H₈.
  • Step 1 – Write the unbalanced equation:
  • C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  • Step 2 – Balance carbon:
  • There are 3 C atoms on the left, so we need 3 CO₂ on the right:
  • C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + H₂O
  • Step 3 – Balance hydrogen:
  • There are 8 H atoms on the left, so we need 4 H₂O (4 × 2 H = 8):
  • C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
  • Step 4 – Balance oxygen:
  • On the right: 3CO₂ = 6 O atoms, 4H₂O = 4 O atoms → total 10 O atoms.
  • To get 10 O atoms from O₂, we need 5 O₂ molecules:
  • C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
  • Final check: all atoms are balanced → equation is complete.
9) Example 2 – Complete Combustion of Nonane
  • Nonane has the formula C₉H₂₀.
  • Step 1 – Write the unbalanced equation:
  • C₉H₂₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  • Step 2 – Balance carbon:
  • 9 C atoms on the left → need 9 CO₂ on the right.
  • Step 3 – Balance hydrogen:
  • 20 H atoms on the left → need 10 H₂O on the right (10 × 2 = 20).
  • Step 4 – Balance oxygen:
  • Right side: 9CO₂ = 18 O atoms; 10H₂O = 10 O atoms → total 28 O atoms.
  • To get 28 O atoms from O₂, we need 14 O₂ molecules.
  • Balanced equation:
  • C₉H₂₀ + 14O₂ → 9CO₂ + 10H₂O
10) Key Summary
  • The properties of alkanes depend on carbon chain length:
  • Shorter alkanes → lower boiling points, more volatile, more flammable.
  • Longer alkanes → higher boiling points, more viscous, less flammable.
  • Hydrocarbons used as fuels undergo combustion.
  • Complete combustion needs enough oxygen and produces CO₂ and H₂O, releasing lots of energy (exothermic).
